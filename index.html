BCG keeps first-order policy gradients usable at stiff, deployable contact — so dynamic humanoid motions learned in differentiable simulation transfer zero-shot to a real Unitree G1.

Abstract

Differentiable simulation provides analytic gradients of robot dynamics, enabling fast and sample-efficient first-order policy optimization. However, obtaining smooth and informative gradients through rigid-body contact typically requires softened contact models, often at the expense of physical fidelity and thereby limiting learned policies largely to simulation. This trade-off becomes particularly consequential for dynamic humanoid motions, where accurate contact dynamics are critical for transferring policies to the real world.

Increasing contact stiffness in rigid-body simulation improves the fidelity of interactions, but also makes the dynamics increasingly sensitive to small state perturbations, producing high-variance gradients that can destabilize first-order policy learning. To address this, we propose Bundled Contact Gradients (BCG), a contact-local randomized smoothing framework for differentiable policy learning. When stiff contact is detected, our method evaluates a local bundle of randomized perturbation rollouts around the stiff contact configuration and aggregates their gradient signal thereby reducing gradient variance.

We demonstrate the effectiveness of our method by successfully training and transferring dynamic motions zero-shot onto a real-world Unitree G1 humanoid platform.

How Bundled Contact Gradients work

Stiff contact is what makes a policy deployable — and what makes its gradient unreliable. At a stiff contact event the derivative of a single trajectory is highly sensitive to small perturbations of the contact configuration, so nearby rollouts disagree sharply about which direction improves the return. BCG keeps the forward dynamics stiff and repairs only the backward pass, by averaging sensitivities over a small bundle of nearby contact configurations.

Crucially, the smoothing is local: bundling is triggered only when a contact normal impulse exceeds a detection threshold \(\tau\), and the branches are advanced in parallel on the GPU. The overhead is therefore limited and incurred exactly where the first-order gradient is most fragile.

Method overview — BCG inside a SHAC-style loop with ADD (paper Fig. 2) static/images/method_overview.png

Policy learning with BCG. The policy drives parallel rollouts. At stiff contact, \(B\) perturbed branches advance for \(H\) steps under shared policy actions and are averaged to continue the rollout. ADD compares simulated and reference motion features to provide imitation rewards, whose gradients propagate through the dynamics to update the policy.

1

Contact-local perturbation

When the set of contacts whose normal impulse exceeds \(\tau\) is non-empty, we perturb the contacting kinematic chain in Cartesian space, so the perturbation scale has a direct physical meaning, and map the offsets back to joint space through the damped pseudoinverse of the Jacobian \(J_k\):

\[\begin{aligned} \Delta p^{(i)} &\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_p^2 I),\\[1pt] \Delta v^{(i)} &\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_v^2 I),\\[1pt] \Delta q^{(i)} &= J_k^{\dagger}\Delta p^{(i)},\\[1pt] \Delta \dot q^{(i)} &= J_k^{\dagger}\Delta v^{(i)}. \end{aligned}\]

Each offset is treated as a constant in the backward pass, so no gradient flows through the sampling itself.

2

Multi-step bundled rollout

The \(B\) perturbed branches evolve for \(H\) control steps under shared actions, then are aggregated back into a single representative state that continues the nominal rollout:

\[\begin{aligned} x^{(i)}_{t+1} &= f_\kappa\!\left(x^{(i)}_t, a_t\right),\\[2pt] \bar{x}_{t+1} &= \mathcal{A}\!\left(x^{(1)}_{t+1},\dots,x^{(B)}_{t+1}\right). \end{aligned}\]

Even \(H=1\) already allows \(S\) physics substeps of branch evolution before the average supplies the reward and the next action. The forward rollout therefore stays stiff and deployable.

3

Gradient propagation

Because the aggregation is an arithmetic mean over branches, the state sensitivity that leaves the bundle is an average of per-branch sensitivities:

\[\bar{Z}_{t+1} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B} \left(F_{x,t}Z^{(i)}_t + F_{a,t}U_t\right).\]

This attenuates sharp, contact-dependent variations by combining sensitivities from nearby trajectories, while the shared action sensitivity \(U_t\) retains the policy's feedback through all \(H\cdot S\) simulation substeps.

The underlying simulator uses the analytically smoothed compliant contact model of Schwarke et al., in which contact impulses are scaled by penetration depth \(d\) through \(s_\kappa(d) = \bigl(1+\exp(-\kappa d)\bigr)^{-1}\). Larger \(\kappa\) approaches the hard-contact response and raises physical fidelity; throughout this work stiff contact means a large but finite \(\kappa\), which keeps the dynamics differentiable while BCG handles the variance it creates. We implement BCG in NVIDIA Warp and train with a SHAC-style actor–critic, using Adversarial Differential Discriminators (ADD) for the motion-imitation reward.

Learned motions in simulation

We train policies to imitate four dynamic, contact-rich motions from the LAFAN1 dataset — Run, Jump, Fight and Dance, each lasting 15 s — on the Unitree G1 humanoid at deployable contact stiffness.

Policy-gradient variance during training. Summed sample variance of all policy-gradient components across the 128 training environments (shading: ± 1 std over 4,000 resamples). Quantitative results

Contact stiffness controls transfer

All policies are trained with SHAC+BCG at the indicated stiffness \(\kappa\) and evaluated over 5 runs after transfer to MuJoCo. Falls counts the runs in which root height drops below 0.3 m; Δd is the difference in mean foot penetration depth [mm] between the training simulator and MuJoCo, where positive values indicate deeper penetration during training. As stiffness rises, both go down: higher physical fidelity in the differentiable simulation transfers more reliably.
